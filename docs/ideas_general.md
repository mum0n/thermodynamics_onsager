Spatiotemporal Bayesian State-Space Modeling for Ecological Thermodynamic Dynamics: Turing.jl Implementation and Onsager Conductivity Matrix Estimation

1. Theoretical & Mathematical Foundations: Thermodynamic States and Latent Process Dynamics

Ecological thermodynamics seeks to unify macroscopic energy flows with microscopic structural variability to establish a physics-based framework for ecosystem directionality, stability, and response to perturbations. However, inferring the fundamental thermodynamic conductivities of ecosystems from field survey data is complicated by observational noise, gear selectivity, and complex spatial process dynamics. Disentangling latent spatiotemporal biological dynamics from observational uncertainty is therefore a strategic necessity for recovering true thermodynamic parameters—such as the phenomenological conductivity matrix—from spatial sampling grids.

To construct a mathematically rigorous thermodynamic framework for open ecological systems, we must address a critical limitation of classical macroscopic thermodynamics. Standard thermodynamic accounting assumes a constant system volume (V). In living ecological systems, however, macroscopic biovolume fluctuates across multiple orders of magnitude due to population growth, spatial migration, and organismal decay. As established by Choi & Hansell (2023, Footnote 5), applying macroscopic (extensive) thermodynamics directly to systems with dynamic biovolume expansion and contraction introduces fundamental ambiguities. To overcome this failure, we adopt an intensive, local thermodynamic formulation based on Prigogine's intensive framework and Landsberg's 4th law formulation of local semi-stable equilibria with small fluctuations from reference states. The change in local specific (intensive) entropy \mathrm{d}S^\ddagger within a localized biovolume element is partitioned into internal entropy production and external entropy exchange:

\mathrm{d}S^\ddagger = \mathrm{d}_i S^\ddagger + \mathrm{d}_e S^\ddagger

where \mathrm{d}_i S^\ddagger \ge 0 represents internal specific entropy production generated by irreversible metabolic processes, respiration, and cellular work, while \mathrm{d}_e S^\ddagger denotes the flux of entropy transported across the boundary of the local biovolume element. Near local steady states, linear phenomenological relations couple thermodynamic fluxes (J_i, representing rates of mass, energy, or metabolic transport per unit biovolume) to conjugate thermodynamic forces (X_j, representing entropic gradients):

J_i = \sum_{j=1}^{K} L_{ij} X_j

where L \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K} is the phenomenological conductivity matrix, defined as the inverse of the resistivity matrix R (L = R^{-1}).

A fundamental property of this linear non-equilibrium regime is the intrinsic self-dampening behavior required by the Second Law of Thermodynamics. The total local entropy production rate per unit biovolume is a positive-definite quadratic form:

\frac{\mathrm{d}_i S^\ddagger}{\mathrm{d}t} = \sum_{i=1}^{K} J_i X_i = \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{j=1}^{K} L_{ij} X_i X_j \ge 0

Because entropy production must remain strictly positive for any non-zero force configuration, the diagonal elements of the phenomenological conductivity matrix must be strictly positive (L_{ii} > 0). Consequently, when an external perturbation introduces a force increment \delta X_i, it drives a conjugate flux increment \delta J_i = L_{ii} \delta X_i. This induced flux acts directly to dissipate the gradient, driving \delta X_i back toward zero. This force-flux dampening loop provides the explicit thermodynamic derivation of the Le Chatelier-Braun principle in ecological systems: local intensive steady states inherently resist external perturbations through diagonal self-dampening conductivities (L_{ii} > 0).

To bridge this thermodynamic formulation with spatiotemporal Bayesian state-space modeling, we define the latent state equation for true ecological log-biomass density \boldsymbol{\chi}_t = [\chi_{t, 1}, \chi_{t, 2}, \dots, \chi_{t, S}]^\top = \log(\mathbf{x}_t) across discrete spatial sites s \in \{1, \dots, S\} and continuous time t \in \{1, \dots, T\}. The latent state vector \boldsymbol{\chi}_t propagates as a multivariate Markovian process driven by the phenomenological conductivity matrix L and spatially correlated process fluctuations:

\boldsymbol{\chi}_t = L \boldsymbol{\chi}_{t-1} + \boldsymbol{\eta}_t, \quad \boldsymbol{\eta}_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_S)

where L \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times S} governs the inter-node or inter-component conductivity interactions, and \boldsymbol{\eta}_t represents Gaussian process innovations constrained by a spatial covariance matrix \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_S.

Moving from aggregate macroscopic (extensive) energy accounting to local intensive representations resolves significant theoretical and empirical challenges. Extensive thermodynamic calculations require specifying global system boundaries and measuring total free energy gradients across open ecosystems where mass-energy cycling spans vast spatial and temporal scales. These ill-defined boundaries make extensive entropy calculations highly ambiguous. In contrast, evaluating local intensive volume elements in quasi-steady state allows us to linearize system dynamics near local reference states under Landsberg's 4th law framework. This mathematical tractability permits the application of Onsager's principle of least specific energy dissipation near steady states, establishing a scale-independent foundation for assessing ecological stability, resistance, and resilience without assuming global boundary closure.

This theoretical formulation of latent process dynamics establishes the mathematical foundation required to model the observational filter and sampling noise.

2. Observation Framework: Disentangling Localized Gear Noise via LogNormal Survey Models

Raw biological survey data collected from spatial field sampling grids cannot be directly substituted into thermodynamic equations. Physical sampling devices—such as bottom trawls, plankton nets, or electrofishing units—introduce substantial observational artifacts, gear selectivity, spatial catchability variations, and zero-inflated or log-normally distributed sampling errors. Using unadjusted survey data to estimate thermodynamic conductivities conflates sampling artifacts with true biological dissipation, distorting the inferred system dynamics.

To isolate localized observation artifacts from latent biological dynamics, we formulate a LogNormal survey observation model. Let y_{t, s} denote the observed biological catch or sample biomass at site s and time t, and let x_{t, s} = \exp(\chi_{t, s}) represent the latent true biological biomass density. The observation likelihood is parameterized on the log-scale to match the latent state vector \chi_{t, s}:

\log y_{t, s} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\chi_{t, s} + \beta_0 + \mathbf{z}_s^\top \boldsymbol{\gamma}, \, \sigma^2_{\text{obs}}\right)

where \beta_0 represents baseline gear catchability/efficiency, \mathbf{z}_s is a site-level covariate vector capturing gear characteristics or micro-habitat variations (e.g., net depth, tow speed, seabed substrate), \boldsymbol{\gamma} is the vector of corresponding covariate coefficients, and \sigma^2_{\text{obs}} isolates localized measurement variance.

The structural distinctions between the underlying thermodynamic process dynamics and the survey observation mechanism are detailed in the comparison below:

Latent Process Dynamics (\chi_{t,s})	Survey Observation Noise (y_{t,s})
Spatial Scale: Continuous regional biomass fields interpolated across sampling grids	Spatial Scale: Point-source, discrete spatial sampling coordinates
Temporal Persistence: Markovian state propagation with system memory governed by matrix L	Temporal Persistence: Transient, epoch-specific observation realizations lacking state memory
Physical Interpretation: True local intensive biological biomass density / specific biovolume	Physical Interpretation: Empirical catch yield modified by gear efficiency and selectivity
Governing Variance: Spatially correlated thermodynamic process innovations (\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_S)	Governing Variance: Uncorrelated localized measurement error and gear noise (\sigma^2_{\text{obs}})

Separating observation noise from latent process dynamics requires constructing continuous spatial covariance matrices to interpolate latent fields across unobserved spatial locations.

3. Spatial Covariance Structure: Gaussian Process Kernels Across Sampling Grids

Field sampling grids routinely feature unobserved spatial locations between discrete stations. Incorporating spatial Gaussian Process (GP) priors into the state-space framework enables spatial interpolation of the latent log-biomass field \boldsymbol{\chi}_t across unobserved spatial coordinates while accounting for regional spatial autocorrelation.

The spatial covariance matrix \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_S \in \mathbb{R}^{S \times S} defined over site coordinates \mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{s}_j \in \mathbb{R}^2 is generated by a spatial covariance kernel K(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{s}_j). We compare two standard spatial kernels:

1. Exponential Kernel: K_E(d) = \sigma_f^2 \exp\left(-\frac{d}{\rho}\right)
2. Matérn (\nu = 3/2) Kernel: K_{M3/2}(d) = \sigma_f^2 \left(1 + \frac{\sqrt{3}d}{\rho}\right) \exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{3}d}{\rho}\right)

where d = \|\mathbf{s}_i - \mathbf{s}_j\|_2 represents Euclidean distance between sites i and j, \sigma_f^2 denotes marginal GP signal variance, and \rho > 0 is the spatial range parameter governing correlation decay over distance.

The mathematical properties of these kernels differ fundamentally in mean-square differentiability and spatial smoothness:

* The Exponential Kernel (equivalent to Matérn \nu = 0.5) generates sample paths that are continuous but nowhere mean-square differentiable. This produces rough spatial fields with rapid spatial fluctuations, which often overrepresent fine-scale spatial noise.
* The Matérn 3/2 Kernel generates sample paths that are once mean-square differentiable. This mathematical property allows the kernel to model continuous, smooth spatial biological gradients without over-smoothing realistic sharp boundaries. Consequently, the Matérn 3/2 kernel provides a realistic prior for continuous ecological fields across spatial sampling grids.

This Bayesian spatiotemporal state-space formulation can be directly implemented using the Turing.jl probabilistic programming framework in Julia.

4. Full Bayesian Turing.jl Implementation & MCMC Workflow

Executing Bayesian state-space inference on high-dimensional latent spatiotemporal models requires efficient gradient-based Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms. Julia, integrated with Turing.jl, provides a high-performance environment for running the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS)—an adaptive variant of Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)—by computing automatic differentiation gradients through complex linear algebra operations.

To ensure computational stability and full posterior parameter tracking, the full S \times S Onsager conductivity matrix L is explicitly parameterized with positive priors on the diagonal (L_{ii} > 0) to enforce Le Chatelier-Braun self-dampening, and asymmetric normal priors on off-diagonal entries (L_{ij}). Latent state vectors \boldsymbol{\chi}_t are sampled directly via multivariate normal state transitions to ensure their full trajectory is recorded in the output chain. Scale consistency is maintained by modeling latent states in log-abundance space (\boldsymbol{\chi}_t) and matching them to the LogNormal likelihood parameters.

using Turing
using LinearAlgebra
using Distances
using Random
using Statistics

# Compute pairwise Euclidean distance matrix across 2D spatial coordinates
function compute_distance_matrix(coords::Matrix{Float64})
    return pairwise(Euclidean(), coords, dims=1)
end

# Evaluate Matern 3/2 Covariance Kernel Matrix
function matern32_kernel(D::Matrix{Float64}, sigma_f::Float64, rho::Float64)
    S = size(D, 1)
    K = zeros(Float64, S, S)
    sqrt3 = sqrt(3.0)
    for i in 1:S
        for j in 1:S
            d = D[i, j]
            K[i, j] = (sigma_f^2) * (1.0 + (sqrt3 * d / rho)) * exp(-(sqrt3 * d / rho))
        end
    end
    return K
end

# Turing Bayesian Spatiotemporal State-Space Model
@model function spatiotemporal_state_space(y::Matrix{Float64}, coords::Matrix{Float64}, z::Vector{Float64}, S::Int, T::Int)
    # Hyperparameter Priors
    rho ~ Gamma(2.0, 1.0)              # Spatial range parameter
    sigma_f ~ Exponential(1.0)         # Marginal GP signal scale
    sigma_obs ~ Exponential(1.0)       # Observation noise scale
    beta0 ~ Normal(0.0, 1.0)           # Baseline gear catchability intercept
    gamma ~ Normal(0.0, 1.0)           # Gear covariate effect coefficient
    
    # Explicit Parameterization of full S x S Conductivity Matrix L
    # Diagonal elements L_ii > 0 enforce Le Chatelier-Braun self-dampening
    L = Matrix{Real}(undef, S, S)
    for i in 1:S
        for j in 1:S
            if i == j
                L[i, j] ~ Exponential(1.0)
            else
                L[i, j] ~ Normal(0.0, 0.5)
            end
        end
    end
    
    # Compute Spatial Distance and GP Covariance Structure
    D = compute_distance_matrix(coords)
    K = matern32_kernel(D, sigma_f, rho)
    K_eps = K + 1e-6 * I               # Numerical jitter for Cholesky stability
    
    # Vectorized Latent Log-Biomass Trajectory over Time t = 1...T
    chi = Vector{Vector{Real}}(undef, T)
    
    # Initial Latent State Vector Prior (t = 1)
    chi[1] ~ MvNormal(zeros(S), K_eps)

    # Markovian Latent Process Propagation (t = 2...T)
    for t in 2:T
        # Matrix-vector multiplication L * chi[t-1] drives mean state transition
        chi[t] ~ MvNormal(L * chi[t-1], K_eps)
    end

    # LogNormal Observation Likelihood Model
    for t in 1:T
        mu_obs = chi[t] .+ beta0 .+ z .* gamma
        for s in 1:S
            y[t, s] ~ LogNormal(mu_obs[s], sigma_obs)
        end
    end
end

# Execution Script and Convergence Diagnostic Workflow
Random.seed!(42)

# Generate Synthetic Field Sampling Data (S sites, T time steps)
S_sites = 4
T_times = 6
coords_data = rand(S_sites, 2) .* 10.0
gear_covariate = rand(S_sites)
y_obs = rand(T_times, S_sites) .+ 0.5

# Instantiate Turing Model
model_instance = spatiotemporal_state_space(y_obs, coords_data, gear_covariate, S_sites, T_times)

# Execute NUTS Sampler
chain = sample(model_instance, NUTS(0.8), 1000)

# Display Full Posterior Summary Statistics
println("=== Posterior Summary Statistics ===")
stats = summarystats(chain)
println(stats)

# Extract Estimates for Conductivity Matrix Parameters and Convergence Diagnostics (R-hat)
println("\n=== Estimated Conductivity Matrix (L) Parameters & Gelman-Rubin Diagnostic (R-hat) ===")
for i in 1:S_sites
    for j in 1:S_sites
        param_symbol = Symbol("L[$i,$j]")
        if param_symbol in chain.name_map.parameters
            mean_val = mean(chain[param_symbol])
            rhat_val = rhat(chain[param_symbol])
            println("L[$i,$j]: Posterior Mean = $(round(mean_val, digits=4)), R-hat = $(round(rhat_val, digits=4))")
        end
    end
end


The joint posterior distributions generated by this MCMC workflow are directly passed to downstream thermodynamic analysis to extract the Onsager conductivity matrix and compute non-equilibrium asymmetry diagnostics.

5. Thermodynamic Parameter Extraction: Onsager Conductivity Matrix (L) & Asymmetry Index

Posterior samples of the latent spatiotemporal dynamics allow us to extract the empirical Onsager conductivity matrix L. In ecological thermodynamic system models, L maps how thermodynamic forces (gradients in specific entropy or biomass density relative to mean reference states) drive biological fluxes. A key insight established by Choi & Hansell (2023) is that when multi-species interaction coefficients are normalized by specific metabolic rates and expressed as local intensive entropy production per unit biomass relative to reference states, the ecological Community Matrix mathematically converges with the Onsager conductivity matrix L.

To evaluate microscopic reversibility and reciprocal symmetry across posterior conductivity estimates, we define the Onsager Asymmetry Index (A_{ij}) for any pair of ecological components or spatial nodes i and j:

A_{ij} = \frac{L_{ij} - L_{ji}}{|L_{ij}| + |L_{ji}|}

The metric A_{ij} provides a quantitative diagnostic threshold:

* Reciprocal Symmetry (A_{ij} \approx 0 \implies L_{ij} = L_{ji}): Indicates strict Onsager reciprocity, where cross-coupling between components is equal and symmetric near local steady states.
* Strong Directionality / Asymmetry (A_{ij} \to \pm 1): Indicates an asymmetric, directional interaction where one component strongly drives or exploits another without equal reciprocal feedback.

Metabolic Allometry and R/B Trade-Offs

The magnitude of the diagonal conductivities L_{ii} is constrained by fundamental physiological allometry. Respiration rate (R, serving as a direct proxy for specific entropy production \mathrm{d}_i S^\ddagger / \mathrm{d}t) and biomass (B, serving as a proxy for specific biovolume) scale allometrically: R \propto B^\alpha (Choi & Hansell 2023, Fig. 2). This allometric constraint establishes a fundamental thermodynamic trade-off between resilience and resistance:

* High R/B Ratios (High Conductivity L_{ii}): Systems characterized by high specific dissipation per unit biomass exhibit high resilience (rapid recovery speed following perturbations) but low resistance (small biomass buffering capacity to absorb external forces).
* Low R/B Ratios (Low Conductivity L_{ii}): Systems characterized by low specific dissipation per unit biomass exhibit high resistance (large biomass buffering capacity capable of dampening large forces) but low resilience (slower recovery speed back to reference states).

System adaptive capacity is defined by the balance between biomass resistance and metabolic resilience under specific thermodynamic constraints.

Ecological Mechanics & Diagnostic Applications

The structural configuration of the matrix L and the asymmetry index A_{ij} directly reflect underlying ecological mechanics:

* Symmetric Coupling (A_{ij} \approx 0): Maps to mutualistic symbioses, synergistic interactions, or tightly coupled feedback loops near local steady states. Perturbations to one component induce reciprocal adjustments in conjugate components, stabilizing the system via Le Chatelier-Braun self-dampening.
* Asymmetric Conductivities (A_{ij} \neq 0): Reflect directional ecological forcing, such as predator-prey exploitation, host-parasite dynamics, or driven environmental forcing. Asymmetries in interaction timescales between species manifest directly as structural asymmetry in L.
* Breaking of Conjugate Effects: Severe environmental stress, habitat destruction, or anthropogenic overexploitation can break conjugate coupling pathways within L. The thermodynamic and dynamical consequences of breaking conjugate effects include:
  * "Order Through Fluctuation" and Bifurcation Dynamics: When external stress breaks reciprocal dampening pathways (L_{ij} \neq L_{ji}), internal microscopic fluctuations amplify beyond the system's dampening capacity. In accordance with Prigogine's principle of "Order through fluctuation," these amplified fluctuations push the system past critical thermodynamic tipping points, triggering a bifurcation into alternative non-equilibrium attractors. A real-world example is the collapse and non-recovery of the Northwest Atlantic cod ecosystem, where overexploitation permanently altered the conjugate interaction pathways.
  * Altered Adaptive Capacity & Metabolic Collapse: Uncoupling conjugate transport processes destroys the balance between respiration R and biomass B, collapsing the biomass buffering capacity and forcing the system into high-dissipation, unstable operational states.
  * Loss of Self-Dampening Resistance: Disruption of reciprocal conductivities removes internal Le Chatelier-Braun dampening. Consequently, minor environmental perturbations trigger amplified system-wide oscillations rather than being safely dissipated.

Applying spatial Bayesian state-space models to field survey data enables the transformation of noisy observation grids into structured, thermodynamic representations of ecosystem stability, adaptive capacity, and regime shifts.
